# Reference — Historical Toponymy & Boundary-History Dating Aid

> Distilled from Edney (1997) *Mapping an Empire*; Barber & Harper (2010) *Magnificent Maps*; Barber (ed.) (2005) *The Map Book*. Operationalised by the `toponym_lookup` tool and the `ToponymyBoundaryAdvisor`.

## Core principle
A place-name on a map is a **terminus post quem** for that name and a **terminus ante quem** for its successor. Political boundaries (colony vs independent state) add a second dating axis.

## Dated toponym changes (reference set)

|---|---|---|---|
| New York City | New Amsterdam → New York | 1664 | "New Amsterdam" = 1624–1664 |

| Sri Lanka | Ceylon → Sri Lanka | 1972 | "Ceylon" = 1815–1972 |

| St Petersburg | St Petersburg → Petrograd → Leningrad → St Petersburg | 1914 / 1924 / 1991 | Petrograd=1914–24, Leningrad=1924–91 |

| Dutch Republic | United Provinces → Batavian Republic → Kingdom of Holland | 1795 / 1806 | United Provinces = 1581–1795 |
| Beijing | Peking → Beijing (pinyin) | c.1979 | Western "Peking" ≈ pre-c.1979 |

## Operational checklist
1. Transcribe **all** place-names on the map (verso and recto).
2. Cross-check each against known rename dates (table above + regional sources).
3. Note **political status**: colony, viceroyalty, republic, kingdom — each has a dated window (e.g. "New Spain" implies 1521–1821).
4. Use the **latest-datable toponym** as the strongest single clue, but require consistency (one anachronistic name may signal a later re-issue or a copy).
5. Combine with boundary history: a frontier line only exists between two dated political entities.

## Guardrail
A single anachronistic toponym may indicate a copy, a re-issue, or a later annotation — not necessarily a forgery. Refer to a specialist for authentication.
